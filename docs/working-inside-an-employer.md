# Working Inside an Employer

Maestro was written for a solo founder. It works just as well inside a job,
as long as you keep one boundary clear: **what is yours and what is your
employer's.** This doc sets that boundary before the first day, not after
the first problem.

---

## The Two Layers

| Layer | What it holds | Where it lives | Who owns it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Public base** | This repo: rhythm, workflow, generic templates | Public GitHub, MIT licence | You. Anyone, your employer included, may use it under the licence. |
| **Work layer** | Plans, briefs, reports, KPIs, contacts, anything about the employer's products, customers or data | The employer's own systems: a private repo in their workspace, or their document platform | The employer |

You clone or copy the public base into the work layer. From that point the
copy is a work file like any other.

## The One-Way Rule

> **Public flows in. Nothing flows back out.**

- Anything you improve on work time, on work equipment, or with work
  information stays in the work layer — even if it looks generic.
- Never copy work material back into the public repo, a personal repo, or a
  personal account, unless the employer agrees in writing.
- Generic improvements to the public base are made on your own time, on your
  own machine, and never derived from something you learned at work.

Most employment contracts give the employer what you create as part of the
job. The one-way rule keeps that clean in both directions: their work stays
theirs, and what you brought in stays yours.

## Accounts and Tools

- **Company accounts only** on company equipment: the AI assistant, the code
  host, the editor's sign-in. Never sign a personal account into work data.
- **Ask before you install.** Most companies have an approved-tools list and
  an open-source policy. Ask what is approved before you clone anything.
- **If cloning is blocked**, the base is markdown and templates. Recreate the
  folders by hand in whatever tool the company approves.
- **Never bring personal projects onto work equipment.** Not a clone, not a
  copy, not a snippet. Mixing them is how ownership becomes an argument.

## What to Ask in the First Weeks

Ask through your manager, framed as "how can I work faster", not "can I
install my tools":

1. Which AI assistants are approved, and on which accounts?
2. Can I install a code editor?
3. Is there a company code host or workspace where I can keep a private repo
   for my own working tools?
4. Can I use open-source tools from public repos, or does that need approval?
5. Where should work documents and data live?

## The Role Plan Cycle

A new role starts with a 90-day plan. After day 90 it becomes a cycle:

```text
Year     ── role goals and KPIs agreed with your manager, plus the year's calendar
 └ Quarter ── three objectives drawn from the year
    └ Week   ── weekly brief and your one-on-one update
       └ Day    ── Top 10 and log

Running through all of it: the results log → reviews → pay and next role
```

| When | What the plan becomes | What it feeds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Days 1–90 | The 90-day plan: learn, deliver, own | The KPIs you agree with your manager |
| Day 90 onward | Quarterly plans: three objectives, reviewed each quarter | Your one-on-ones |
| End of probation | The results log so far | The case for an early adjustment |
| Each year | The annual review: last year's results, next year's goals | The pay review |
| Each year | The annual calendar, rebuilt from last year's reports | Better plans, from your own data |
| Year two onward | A development plan: the next role and the skills it needs | Promotion conversations |

**The results log holds it together.** It is written every week, so every
review draws on a record instead of on memory.
